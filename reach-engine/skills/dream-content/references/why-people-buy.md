# Why people buy

Read this when you're helping them:
- pick or sharpen a product idea (Define, round 6)
- write their promise (Define, round 7, and the Research verdict)
- decide what goes inside their product (Execute)
- write anything a buyer will read: titles, descriptions, mockup text, sales pages, emails, posts (Apply and Market). Sections 14 (plain, specific words) and 15 (keywords) apply to every one of these.

These ideas come from how bigger, pricier programs are sold. They're adapted here for a beginner's first digital product, usually $5 to $50.
Use them quietly to make your suggestions better. Bring one idea to the student only when it helps the decision in front of them, in one or two plain lines.
Never teach this page as a lesson.

## Words to use with them

| Don't say | Say |
|---|---|
| offer | your product |
| avatar, ICP, target market | your buyer, who it's for |
| deliverables, value stack | what's inside |
| promise statement, USP | your promise, what makes yours different |
| pain point | what's hard for them right now |
| dream outcome | what they want instead |
| CTA | the last line that tells them what to do next |
| conversion | people buying |
| risk reversal, scarcity, high ticket, low ticket | (don't use these at all) |

## 1. People buy what they already say they want

People don't pay for our passion. They pay to fix something they already know is a problem, and they describe it in their own words.
- Build from what buyers say (Research collects it), not from what you or the student think buyers need.
- If buyers say "our money is gone before payday", the product is about a plan for every paycheck. Not about "a healthier relationship with money".
- When the student's idea and the buyer's words don't match, side with the buyer's words, and say so kindly.

## 2. A result they can see beats a feeling

Buyers need to picture the "after".
Feelings like "more confident", "less overwhelmed", "your best self" or "unlock your potential" mean something different to every person, so they don't sell.
Turn a feeling into something they could see, count or finish:
1. Picture the buyer using the product.
2. Ask: what would look different in their day? (A plan on the fridge. Dinner on the table by 6. A finished budget. Bedtime with no fight.)
3. Pick the one that matters most to this buyer, and lead with it.

| A feeling | Something they can see |
|---|---|
| Less stressed about dinner | A week of dinners planned in 15 minutes |
| More organized | Every bill and due date on one page |
| Confident on camera | Your first 10 Reels planned and scripted |
| Calmer mornings | Kids out the door on time with one checklist |

The feeling can still live in the description, after the visible result.

## 3. The big reasons people spend money

Most buying comes back to a few things people have always wanted:
- **Money:** earning it, saving it, keeping track of it.
- **Looks and health:** feeling good in their body, fitness, skincare, food.
- **Love and family:** their partner, their kids, their home, their wedding.
- **Relief:** less pain, less stress, less time lost to something hard.

For small digital products, relief sells well, as long as the relief is something they can see (section 2).
Use this as a quick check on a product idea: which of these does it touch?
If none, help them connect it to one. A Canva template for coaches isn't really about design. It's about saving time, or bringing in clients.

## 4. What makes it feel worth more

A product feels more valuable when:
1. **The result is bigger,** or matters more to them.
2. **It's more likely to work for them:** made for their exact situation, with an example and clear steps.
3. **It's faster.**
4. **It takes less effort.**

Ways a digital product can turn each of these up:
- Made for one specific buyer (a budget planner for moms whose money is gone before payday, not "a budget planner").
- A filled-in example page next to each blank one.
- A short "start here" page: how to use it in the first 10 minutes.
- Parts done for them: pre-filled lists, sample scripts, ready-made captions.
- Fewer steps. If it takes 20 pages to explain, it's probably two products.

Use these when suggesting what goes inside (section 6) and when pricing (Research). A product that does more of these can sit higher in the price range.

## 5. Their promise

Their promise is one sentence that says who it's for and what changes for them.
Everything else (title, description, mockups, posts) is a version of it.

In Define, it starts in Kristin's format: **Before my product, they ___. After using it, they ___.**
In Research, sharpen it into their **promise sentence**, using the buyer's own words. Use at least three of these:
- **Who:** their buyer, as specific as possible (moms whose money is gone before payday, new Etsy sellers, brides on a budget).
- **What they get:** something they can see (section 2).
- **A number,** only if it's true. Numbers about the product and the time it takes are safest: 7 dinners, 30 posts, 1 page, 15 minutes.
- **How long:** in one weekend, in 15 minutes a week.
- **Without ___:** the thing they dread or think they have to do (cooking twice, starting from a blank page, hiring a designer). Research collects these.

Examples:
- Every bill planned before payday, in 15 minutes a month, without a spreadsheet.
- Your first 30 Instagram posts planned in one weekend, without staring at a blank screen.
- Your whole wedding budget on one page, without a spreadsheet.

Check it:
- **One promise.** No "and" or "or". Two promises means two products, or a choice they haven't made yet.
- **Plain words** a stranger understands the same way. No "best self", "next level", "unlock", "transform your life".
- **One breath.** The short version should be about 60 characters.
- **Their buyer's words,** not marketing words.

**Honest promises (always):**
- The promise must be something the product really does.
- Never promise a buyer's income, weight loss, health result, or a "guaranteed" outcome. A planner can promise a plan, not a body or a paycheck.
- No fake urgency or scarcity on a digital download: no "only 3 left", no countdowns, no "price goes up tonight" unless it truly does.
- No money-back promise unless they've decided to honor it. Most digital downloads don't offer refunds. That decision belongs to Apply.

## 6. What's inside

What's inside is how the buyer gets to the promise. It's not the promise.
- **5 or 6 parts at most.** Each one must help the promise come true, or remove one thing that would stop the buyer. If a part does neither, cut it.
- **One bonus at most,** and make it useful (a quick-start page, a filled-in example, a printable cheat sheet).
- **Always include a way to start fast:** a "start here" page or a filled-in example (section 4).
- **Lead with the 2 or 3 parts buyers care about most.** Those go in the title, the first line of the description and the main mockup. The full list goes further down. A long list up front overwhelms people, and they start counting the parts they won't use.
- **Pick the easiest format for this buyer:** printable, fillable PDF, Canva template or Google Sheet. A busy mom on her phone and a teacher at a laptop want different things.

## 7. One promise, many places

Once the promise sentence is approved, write a version of it for each place. Same promise, just shorter or longer.
Where people search (Etsy, Pinterest), keep the buyer's search words first. For hooks and opening lines, use the formulas in `hooks.md`.

| Where | Shape | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Etsy title | {search phrase} for {who}, {what they get}, {format} | Budget Planner Printable for Moms, Plan Every Paycheck, Monthly Bill Tracker |
| First line of the description | {what they get}, without {what they dread} | Plan every bill before payday, without a spreadsheet. |
| Main mockup headline | the result, in 5 words or fewer | Every bill, planned. |
| Pinterest pin title | {search phrase}: {what they get} | Budget Planner Printable: Plan Every Paycheck in 15 Minutes |
| Instagram or Facebook hook | talk straight to {who} about {what's hard} | If your money is gone before payday, this is for you. |
| Email subject line | {what they get} in {how long} | Dinner, planned in 15 minutes |
| The last line (what to do next) | short and plain | Get the planner |

## 8. Keep improving (after they launch)

- Change one thing at a time (the title, the main photo or the price), so they can tell what made the difference.
- Ask buyers what they used most and what they'd add. Their answers beat any guess, including yours.
- The words people use change over time. Keep collecting them.

## 9. The gap: where they are now, and where they want to be

Every buyer is somewhere they don't want to be (stuck, tired, overwhelmed) and wants to be somewhere better. The product is the bridge. Selling is simply showing the bridge clearly.
- **Where they are now:** say it in their own words from Research, so they feel seen. ("The money's gone before payday.")
- **Where they want to be:** visible and specific (section 2). ("Every bill planned before payday.")
- Kristin's Before/After promise is this gap in one sentence. Every title, description and image should make the gap and the bridge obvious.

## 10. Real urgency, never fake urgency

People put off fixing things for months, even years. What moves them is seeing what staying the same is costing them, not pressure.
- **Real urgency** is the honest cost of staying stuck, in their words: "another month of running out before payday", "another month of guessing what to post". Name it gently, once. Never shame them, and never exaggerate it.
- **Fake urgency** is pressure we invent: countdowns that reset, "only 3 left" on a download, a price that "goes up tonight" when it doesn't. It feels salesy, it leads to regret, and it's banned in our rules.
- A real deadline is fine **only if it's true** (for example a launch price that really ends after the first 10 sales).

## 11. Who it's for, and who it's not for

Being clear about who it isn't for makes the right buyer trust it more, and keeps the wrong buyer from regretting it.
- Add a short **"This is for you if..."** and **"This isn't for you if..."** to descriptions and sales pages. ("This isn't for you if your child has a diagnosed feeding disorder. Please talk to a specialist.")
- Never try to sell to someone it won't help. A sale that doesn't help them isn't a win.

## 12. Selling without feeling salesy

Many beginners feel pushy the moment they share their product. Help them see it differently, in a line or two, when it comes up:
- If the product truly helps, **sharing it is helping.** The mom checking her bank app in the grocery line tonight would be glad to find it.
- That only works if the product is good. So we make it genuinely useful first (Execute), then share it with confidence.
- Warm and real beats polished and perfect. Their own story, in their own voice, is what makes people trust them.

## 13. Their next product

The best next product solves **the next problem for the same buyer**, not a new problem for a new audience.
- After the budget planner works, the same mom might want a grocery budget tracker or a debt payoff tracker. That's a natural next product, or a bundle.
- Keep the buyer the same until the first product sells. One buyer, one problem, one solution at a time.
- Note next-product ideas in their Decisions when they come up. They belong in the product's "What's next" page later (on Etsy, pointing only to their Etsy shop).

## 14. Plain, specific words (Kristin's findings)

Everything a buyer reads (titles, descriptions, pages, emails, posts) follows these rules:
- **Write at a 4th or 5th grade reading level.** People read simple words faster, and they're scrolling past a lot of noise. Short words, short sentences. Simple isn't dumbing down: it shows you know the topic well.
- **Every sentence must earn its place.** Kristin calls it weighing the sentence: take a sentence out, and if the message still works, leave it out.
- **Watch for "and".** It often hides two ideas. Split it into two short sentences, or pick the stronger idea.
- **Say things they could check or picture.** Write "dinner on the table by 6", not "a happier evening". Vague words make the reader guess, and they guess the worst. Kristin's restaurant menu example: "steak: expensive" sells nothing, but the cut, the sides and the price sell the dish.
- **Paint one scene, like a movie.** Pick the one pain that matters most, show one real moment of it in their day, then show the opposite: the same moment after the product.
- **Cut these words, or replace them with something concrete:** many, several, helps, typically, should, high quality, amazing, really, impactful, valuable.
  - Not useful: "Many moms find this really helpful."
  - Better: "A week of dinners on one page."
- **Use the buyer's own words** from Research, as if they're sitting across the table. Never talk down to them.
- **Stories sell, but only true ones.** Their own story, or a real customer's (shared with permission). Never a made-up "customer story".
- **Sound like them.** Read it out loud. If they wouldn't say it that way, change it. A little personality (a "honestly?", a "boom!") is fine if it's really how they talk.
- **Copy can't create the want, only focus it.** Lead with what buyers already said they want (section 1). Don't try to convince them they have a problem.

## 15. Keywords: one set, used everywhere it counts

Their keywords are the words buyers actually type. Research collects them (`step-research.md`, part 4). Before writing anything a buyer will see, pull these from their notes:
- **Main keyword:** the one phrase their product is named for ("budget planner printable").
- **3 to 5 supporting phrases:** close variations buyers type ("monthly budget planner", "paycheck budget planner", "budget planner for moms").
- **The buyer's phrases:** how they describe the problem in their own words ("the money's gone before payday").
- **Phrases to avoid:** too broad, or the wrong buyer (for a budget planner for moms: "business budget", "college budget", "accounting").

**Where each one goes:**

| Place | What to put there |
|---|---|
| Product name and file name | The main keyword first: `Budget-Planner-Printable.pdf` |
| Shop name, shop title, slogan, banner, About, bio | **The whole niche, never one product.** Use a broad niche word buyers use ("budget"), plus who it's for. For example: "Simple budget printables for busy moms". It has to fit every product they'll ever add. |
| Listing or product title | Narrowed to the one product: the main keyword first, then who it's for and the result, in natural words. On Etsy, one or two supporting phrases too. |
| First line of the description | The main keyword in a normal sentence, within the first two lines. Search engines and marketplaces show this part. |
| Etsy tags (13) | The main keyword plus supporting and buyer phrases, up to 20 characters each, with no tag repeated word for word. |
| Sales page | The call-out line names the buyer. The headline is the result in plain words. The main keyword goes in the subheadline and the first lines. |
| Page settings (where the platform has them) | SEO title and description, and the web address (`.../budget-planner-printable`). |
| Images | File names and alt text that describe the picture with the keyword ("budget planner printable monthly page"). |
| Mockup text | The result in 5 words or fewer. A keyword only if it fits naturally. |
| Pins, posts and emails (Market) | Pins and pin descriptions start with the keyword. Captions and emails use the buyer's phrases more than keywords. |

**Let them see the list at work.** The first time you use their keywords in a step (a title, tags, pins), say so in a few words, like "using your keyword list from Research". If they ask for their keywords ("Show my keywords"), show the set from their notes plus the full list Research found.

**Rules:**
- **Write for people first.** A keyword must read naturally, or it doesn't go in. Never a pile of keywords.
- **One main keyword per product or page.** The same phrase goes in the title, the first line and the images, so the page is clearly about one thing.
- **Only phrases from real sources:** search-bar suggestions, real listing titles, the buyer's own quotes. Never a made-up search volume.
- **Leave out phrases for the wrong buyer,** even popular ones. The right 50 visitors beat the wrong 500.
